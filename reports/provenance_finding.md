# Entity-level provenance checking is neither sound nor complete for clinical faithfulness

**A primary result of the PharmaGuard project.**
Date: 2026-07-24 · All evidence below is reproducible from the repository.

---

## Summary

**Automated verification of clinical faithfulness has structural limits that
differ by claim type.** We tested three independent approaches against real
generated clinical text. Each failed in a different way, and the three failures
are not variations of one bug — they are three distinct boundaries.

| # | Approach | How it failed | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| **a** | Lexical term-overlap | Unsound *and* incomplete: passed a polarity-reversed claim, failed a faithful paraphrase | **15/16** failures were false positives |
| **b** | Assertion-marker rules | Structurally blind to a fabricated *mechanism* — no number, no duration, no known concept, so nothing fires | 1 named miss, unfixable within the approach |
| **c** | Closed-vocabulary (nouns vs corpus) | Catches the fabricated mechanism, but penalises plain-language explanation of a technical corpus | **57%** FP, **30%** after narrowing |

Approach (c) was narrowed with a decision rule recorded *before* tuning — keep
below 15% false positives, retire at or above. It reached 30%. It was retired
from the gate rather than tuned further, because the first detector had already
been tuned 12 → 4 → 0 and was blunted in the process.

**The conclusion is not that verification is hopeless.** Each check is genuinely
useful within its boundary: entity-level guarding catches fabricated doses,
assertion-level checking catches invented quantities and timelines, polarity
checking catches negation reversal, and vocabulary checking detects foreign
entities. What none of them can do — individually or together — is certify that
a clinical sentence is *correct*.

**So automated checks are triage, and human adjudication is the gate.** The
checks reduce twenty explanations to the sentences a person must examine; the
person decides. With a pre-generated set of twenty that is tractable, which is
the practical payoff of pre-generating the entire explanation space rather than
generating per request. An LLM in the request path forecloses this by
construction: you can review a sample, never the thing the next user receives.

## The checker under test

The full decision was a set-difference over content words:

```python
def traces_to(sentence: str, *sources: str) -> tuple[bool, set[str]]:
    """Does every content word of `sentence` appear in some source?"""
    haystack = " ".join(s or "" for s in sources).lower()
    present = set(re.findall(r"[a-z][a-z0-9\-]*|\d+(?:\.\d+)?", haystack))
    for word in list(present):
        present |= _normalise(word)
    untraced = {
        word for word in content_words(sentence) if not (_normalise(word) & present)
    }
    return (not untraced), untraced
```

There is no representation of a claim, a predicate, negation, or entailment. A
word is either present in the source string or it is not.

## Evidence 1 — unsoundness: a negated claim passes

Source: `"Consider dose reduction."`

| Candidate | Should be | Checker said |
| --- | :---: | :---: |
| `Consider dose reduction.` (verbatim) | PASS | PASS |
| **`Do NOT consider dose reduction.`** | **FAIL** | **PASS** ❌ |

The negation inverts the clinical instruction — from reducing a dose to not
reducing it — while reusing every word. A checker built on vocabulary overlap
cannot see the difference. In a pharmacogenomic context this is the most
consequential error available: it is the difference between reducing a
thiopurine dose in a poor metaboliser and not reducing it.

Reproduce: `python scripts/diagnose_provenance.py`
Pinned as a regression test:
`test_provenance_policy.py::TestPolarity::test_the_demonstrated_negation_failure_is_caught`

## Evidence 2 — incompleteness: a faithful paraphrase fails

| Candidate | Should be | Checker said |
| --- | :---: | :---: |
| `Your doctor may lower your dose.` | PASS | **FAIL** ❌ (untraced: `doctor`, `lower`) |

"Lower your dose" and "dose reduction" are the same claim. The words differ, so
the checker rejected it. This is not an edge case: rendering clinical language
into words a patient understands is the entire purpose of the patient-facing
field, and the metric penalised exactly that behaviour.

## Evidence 3 — the false-positive rate on real output

Three explanations generated by `meta/llama-3.1-8b-instruct` (one Safe, one
Adjust Dosage, one Toxic) were scored, and every failure was classified by hand
against its source.

| Classification | Count |
| --- | ---: |
| True positive (genuinely unsourced claim) | **0** |
| Borderline (wording drift) | 1 |
| **False positive** | **15** |

**Not one failure was a fabricated clinical claim.** The causes:

| Cause | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Connective words | *"…removes a brake, **leading** to bone-marrow suppression"* — failed on the conjunction |
| Phenotype descriptors | Failed for naming the phenotype it was **given as input** (`intermediate`, `metabolizer`) |
| The drug's own name | One sentence's only untraced word was `fluorouracil` |
| Plain-language rendering | `doctor`, `body`, `low white blood cell count` (the lay rendering of the sourced term `leukopenia`) |
| Procedural framing | *"Your doctor or pharmacist can help you understand what this means"* — asserts nothing clinical, was scored as a clinical claim |

Full per-sentence detail: `reports/provenance_diagnosis.md`.

## Evidence 4 — (b) assertion rules are blind to fabricated mechanisms

Injected into real generated mechanism prose:

> *"The enzyme is inhibited by grapefruit juice, which raises plasma levels."*

No number. No duration. No unknown concept word. Every assertion rule passed it.
A fabricated causal mechanism can be assembled entirely from ordinary vocabulary,
so a checker built on assertion markers cannot see it — not a tuning gap, a
structural one.

Reproduce: `python scripts/detector_sensitivity.py`

## Evidence 5 — (c) closed vocabulary trades one error for the other

Requiring mechanism nouns to appear in the cited corpus catches the grapefruit
sentence immediately. Applied to the 53 real mechanism sentences actually
generated, it flagged 30 of them — a **57% false-positive rate**. The flagged
terms were `genetic`, `well`, `properly`, `ability`, `effectively`: ordinary
English used to explain a technical corpus in readable prose.

Restricting to POS-tagged concrete nouns (excluding abstract nouns) halved it to
**30%**, still flagging `side`, `blood`, `bloodstream`, `buildup` — again, the
vocabulary of explanation rather than of fabrication.

This is the same tension as (a), reappearing at a narrower scope: any check that
requires shipped words to resemble source words penalises the act of explaining.

| Approach | Catches fabricated mechanism | FP rate on real prose |
| --- | :---: | ---: |
| Assertion markers | ❌ | 0% |
| Closed vocabulary, all content words | ✅ | 57% |
| Closed vocabulary, concrete nouns | ✅ | 30% |

## The replacement: field-level policy

Different fields carry different obligations, so a single global rule was the
wrong shape to begin with.

| Field | Rule | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `clinical_recommendation` | **VERBATIM** | Never model-authored. Byte-identical to PharmCAT output. |
| `summary`, `mechanism`, `variant_rationale` | **CLAIM-LEVEL** | Wording may differ; every clinical *assertion* must trace. |
| `patient_friendly` | **NO NEW CLAIMS** | Paraphrase expected; may not introduce a dose, timeline, probability, comparative risk or mechanism the source lacks. |

Claims are detected by rule, not by a model — using an LLM to judge an LLM is
circular and spends quota to answer a question that documented rules answer more
predictably. A sentence is scanned for **assertion markers**; sentences with
none assert nothing clinical and pass.

- **Literal** assertions (quantities, timelines) must match the source's own
  number *with its unit*. This matters: an early version matched bare numbers
  and let an invented `15% chance` trace to an unrelated `15` in a citation.
- **Conceptual** assertions (direction, harm, severity, mechanism) match against
  documented synonym families, so *lower* matches *reduction*.
- **Polarity** is checked separately and in both directions, closing Evidence 1.
  A distinct prohibition rule catches the case where the source says *avoid* and
  the candidate says *use* — there the candidate contains no negation to compare.

### Result on the same evidence

| Metric | Old checker | Field-level policy |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sentences passing (3 captured outputs) | 11/27 | **27/27** |
| False positives among failures | 15/16 | — |
| Negated claim caught | ❌ no | ✅ yes |
| Faithful paraphrase allowed | ❌ no | ✅ yes |

Reproduce: `python scripts/benchmark_models.py --provider nvidia --rescore`
(no API calls — it re-scores stored output).

## What the new checker still cannot do

Stated plainly, because a check whose limits are undocumented invites being
trusted past its competence:

**It cannot detect a reversed causal claim assembled from sourced concepts.**
*"Reduced TPMT activity lowers TGN accumulation"* is backwards — reduced TPMT
*raises* TGN — but both `direction_down` and `mechanism` appear in the source, so
a concept-level check passes it.

This is pinned as a deliberately-passing test
(`TestDocumentedBlindSpots::test_it_cannot_detect_a_reversed_claim`) which
states that if it ever begins to fail, the documentation must be updated to
match.

## Consequence

1. **Automated provenance checking is triage.** It reduces twenty explanations
   to the handful of sentences a person must examine. That is genuinely useful
   and it is not certification.
2. **Human adjudication is the release gate.** `scripts/adjudicate.py` presents
   each sentence beside its source; `scripts/adjudication_status.py` fails the
   build if any shipped sentence is undecided. Twenty entries is a tractable
   amount to examine once — which is the payoff of pre-generating the entire
   explanation space rather than generating per request. An LLM in the request
   path forecloses this by construction: you can review a sample, never the
   thing the next user actually receives.
3. **A perfect score is a reason for suspicion, not confidence.** The template's
   100% was the first sign that the metric was measuring the wrong thing. Any
   verification metric should be probed with a deliberately wrong input before
   its passing scores are believed.

**This adjudication is not clinical expert review.** This project has no
qualified clinical reviewer and does not claim one. What is recorded is that the
author compared each sentence against its cited source. That is weaker than
clinical review and is never labelled as more.
